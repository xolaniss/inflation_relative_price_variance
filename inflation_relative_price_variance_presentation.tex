\section{Motivation}\label{motivation}

\begin{frame}{Motivation}
\phantomsection\label{motivation-1}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Inflation is known to have adverse welfare effects through its
  distortion of relative prices which causes the misallocation of
  resources, production inefficiencies leading to lower real economic
  activity.
\item
  This is well supported by both theoretical and empirical studies.
\item
  This paper contributes to the empirical literature, by examining the
  relationship between inflation and relative price variability (RPD) in
  South Africa given the little evidence available and to better
  understand how this relationship has evolved and the implications for
  monetary policy.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Motivation}
\phantomsection\label{motivation-2}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The South African context can provide further insights into the
  relationship between inflation and RPD given the shifts in the
  inflation targets from 3-6 per cent in 2000 to 4.5 per cent since 2017
  as structural changes in inflation can affect the link between
  inflation and relative price variability (Caglayan and Filiztekin
  2003)
\item
  That the functional form of relationship is U-shaped and dynamic
  (Fielding and Mizen 2000; Choi 2010)
\item
  The latter motivates the estimation of the inflation rate that
  minimizes relative price distortions since if the relationship was
  linear then the optimal inflation rate would be zero
\item
  Given the inflation target reform towards a 3\% in South Africa, this
  study contributes to the policy discussion on the appropriate
  inflation target and understating how dynamic relations between
  inflation and relative price variability has evolved
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\section{Theoretical Framework: RPD through Lucas's aggregate supply
curve}\label{theoretical-framework-rpd-through-lucass-aggregate-supply-curve}

\begin{frame}{RPD theoretical framework}
\phantomsection\label{rpd-theoretical-framework}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  The theoretical literature generally distinguishes between menu-cost
  and signal extraction models in explaining the positive relation
  between trend inflation and RPD.
\item
  In these models' firms follow a one-sided dynamic pricing rule when
  setting prices and face fixed non-zero costs of adjusting nominal
  prices (e.g.~printing new menus) when inflation increases as the
  latter reduces the optimal real price of goods and services.
\item
  Due to differences in administrative costs, these nominal price
  adjustment costs vary across firms moreover these adjustments occur at
  different times which implies staggard price setting behaviour which
  leads to relative price variability.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RPD theoretical framework}
\phantomsection\label{rpd-theoretical-framework-1}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  Menu cost models predict a that higher expected inflation causes an
  increase in RPD (citations xxx) while Lucas-type signal extraction
  models (Lucas 1973; Hercowitz 1981; Cukierman 1983) argue unexpected
  inflation uncertainty causes higher RPD because informational
  frictions make it difficult to distinguish between idiosyncratic
  relative shocks or an aggregate (inflationary) shock.
\item
  Menu cost and signal-extraction models assume a positive and linear
  relationship making the optimal inflation rate zero, however, recently
  monetary search models (Head and Kumar 2005) show that this
  relationship is nonlinear making the optimal inflation rate somewhere
  between zero and positive inflation rate.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RPD theoretical framework}
\phantomsection\label{rpd-theoretical-framework-2}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  The concept of relative price dispersion can be abstract and difficult
  to grasp without a theoretical framework.
\item
  One of the most influential frameworks for understanding relative
  price dispersion is the Lucas (1973) aggregate supply curve, which
  provides insights into how relative price dispersion can explain the
  output-inflation relationship.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RPD theoretical framework}
\phantomsection\label{rpd-theoretical-framework-3}
\begin{itemize}
\item
  This theory essentially means (in part) that nominal ``rigidities''
  partially dominate short-run supplier behaviour resulting from a lack
  of information about the some prices relevant to decision making.
\item
  For our purposes we ignore other aspects of the theoretical framework
  and emphasis this aspect of the model to provide an understanding
  relative price dispersion.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RPD theoretical framework}
\phantomsection\label{rpd-theoretical-framework-4}
Lucas (1973) proposes a decomposition of the supply curve, all in logs,
into a natural or secular component and a cyclical component as follows:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-lucas_supply_curve}{y_t(z) = y_{n,t} + y_{ct}(z), }\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(y_t(z)\) is the aggregate supply at time \(t\) in market \(z\),
  \(y_{n,t}\) is the natural or secular level of supply at time \(t\)
\item
  \(y_{ct}\) is the cyclical component of supply at time \(t\).
\item
  Competitive markets and a cleared labour market are assumed
\item
  Leading to relative price and general price level movements leading.
\item
  Imperfect information of the suppliers about the general price level
  that leads to relative price dispersion and the output-inflation
  relationship.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RPD theoretical framework}
\phantomsection\label{rpd-theoretical-framework-5}
In addition, the natural or secular level of supply is assumed to follow
a linear trend as follows:
\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-natural_supply}{ y_{n,t} = \alpha + \beta t, }\end{equation}

where \(\alpha\) is a constant and \(\beta\) captures the trend growth
rate of the natural level of supply.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RPD theoretical framework}
\phantomsection\label{rpd-theoretical-framework-6}
The cyclical component varies with the perceived relative price (the
difference between the actual price level and the expected price level)
and its lags as follows:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-cyclical_component}{ y_{ct}(z) = \gamma[P_t(z) - E(P_t | I_t(z))] + \lambda y_{c, t-1}(z), }\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\gamma\) captures the sensitivity of supply to the perceived
  relative price, \(P_t(z)\) is the actual price level in market \(z\)
  at time \(t\),
\item
  \(E(P_t | I_t(z))\) is the expected price level based on information
  available at time \(t\) in market \(z\)
\item
  \(\lambda\) captures the persistence of the cyclical component and
  \(|\lambda| < 1\). \(E(P_t | I_t(z))\) is current expected price level
  conditioned on information that is available at time \(t\), at market
  \(z\).
\item
  The available information set (\(I_t(z)\)) comprises of past demand
  driven shifts of the secular component (\(y_{n,t}\)),
\item
  Past deviations of the cyclical component
  (\(y_{c, t-1}, y_{c, t-2}, ...\)) from its natural level.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RPD theoretical framework}
\phantomsection\label{rpd-theoretical-framework-7}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The current general price level (\(P_t\)), which determines `prior'
  expectations common across all suppliers in all markets, is assumed to
  be \(P_t \sim N(\overline{P_t}, \sigma^2)\)
\item
  \(\overline{P_t}\) is the mean price level at time \(t\) and
  \(\sigma^2\) is the variance of the price level at time \(t\).
\item
  Furthermore, Lucas (1973) defines \(z_t\) as the percentage deviation
  of the actual market price from the general price level as
  \(z\overset{\text{iid}}{\sim} N(0, \tau^2),\) where \(\tau^2\) is the
  variance of \(z\).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RPD theoretical framework}
\phantomsection\label{rpd-theoretical-framework-8}
Therefore, the actual price level in market \(z\) at time \(t\) can be
expressed as:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-price_decomposition}{P_t(z) = P_t + z, }\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(P_t(z)\) is the actual price in market \(z\) at time \(t\) (that is
  the markets are indexed to z),
\item
  \(P_t\) is the current general price level at time \(t\), and \(z\) is
  the percentage deviation of the actual market price from the general
  price level.
\item
  However, \(P_t\) is unobserved by traders, and therefore \(I_t(z)\) is
  used to estimate the expected price level (\(E(P_t| I_t(z))\)).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RPD theoretical framework}
\phantomsection\label{rpd-theoretical-framework-9}
The expected price level based on information available at time \(t\) in
market \(z\) can be derived using Bayes' rule as follows:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-expected_price_level}{ E(P_t| I_t(z))  = E(P_t|P_t(z), \overline{P_t}) = (1-\theta)P_t(z) + \theta\overline{Pt},}\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\theta = \frac{\tau^2}{(\sigma^2 + \tau^2)}\) is the weight placed
  on the mean price level (\(\overline{P_t}\)) in forming expectations.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RPD theoretical framework}
\phantomsection\label{rpd-theoretical-framework-10}
Combining Equation~\ref{eq-lucas_supply_curve},
Equation~\ref{eq-price_decomposition}, and
Equation~\ref{eq-expected_price_level} by substitution yields the
following:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-lucas_supply_curve_final}{ y_t(z) = y_{nt} + \theta \gamma[P_t(z) - \overline{P_t}] + \lambda y_{c, t-1}}\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  current supply in market \(z\) at time \(t\) is a function of the
  natural level of supply, the perceived relative price (the difference
  between the actual price level in market \(z\) and the mean price
  level), and the lagged cyclical component.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RPD theoretical framework}
\phantomsection\label{rpd-theoretical-framework-11}
Finally, aggregating across all markets (or integrating over all
markets) yields the following aggregate supply curve:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-aggregate_supply_curve}{y_t = y_{nt} + \theta\gamma(P_t - \overline{P_t}) + \lambda[y_{t-1} - y_{n, t-1}]}\end{equation}

From Equation~\ref{eq-aggregate_supply_curve}, we can clearly
demonstrate how the relative price dispersion (\(\theta\)) is a fraction
of the total individual price dispersion (\(\sigma^2 + \tau^2\)),
affects the aggregate supply curve, and therefore the output-inflation
relationship.
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{RPD theoretical framework}
\phantomsection\label{rpd-theoretical-framework-12}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  That is, \(\theta \uparrow\) in \(\tau^2\) and \(\downarrow\) in
  \(\sigma^2\).
\item
  Meaning that when \(\theta \uparrow\), the aggregate supply curve
  becomes flatter, and the output-inflation relationship becomes
  stronger.
\item
  And when \(\theta\downarrow\), the slope is restricted by \(\gamma\).
\item
  Therefore, the relative price variance (\(\theta\)), in this context,
  is a key determinant of the slope of the aggregate supply curve and
  the strength of the output-inflation relationship.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Methodology}
\phantomsection\label{methodology}
RPD is calculated in line with Lach and Tsiddon (1992) as the weighted
standard deviation of individual price inflation rates from the
aggregate inflation rate. The RPD is essentially the variability of
relative prices of a given product. - We first calculate the monthly
inflation rate as follows:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-inflation_rate}{ \pi_{i,t} = ln\left(\frac{P_{i,t}}{P_{i,t-1}}\right) * 100, }\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\pi_{i,t}\) is the inflation rate of good \(i\) at time \(t\)
\item
  \(P_{i,t}\) is the price of good \(i\) at time \(t\)
\item
  \(P_{i,t-1}\) is the price of good \(i\) at time \(t-1\)
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Methodology}
\phantomsection\label{methodology-1}
Then, the RPD is calculated as:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-rpd}{RPD_{t} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i}(\pi_{i,t} - \pi_{t})^2}, }\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\omega_{j}\) is the expenditure share of the \(j^{th}\) good,
\item
  \(\pi_{t}\) is the headline or aggregate inflation rate at time \(t\),
\item
  \(\sum_{i=1}^{n}\omega_{i} = 1\).
\item
  The expenditure weights (\(\omega_{i}\)) used are derived from the
  Consumer Price Index (CPI) basket provided by Stats SA
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Methodology}
\phantomsection\label{methodology-2}
To examine the relationship between inflation and RPD, we estimate the
following econometric model:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-rpd_reg}{RPD_{t} = \beta_0 + \beta_1|\pi_{t}| + \sum_{h=1}^{3}\omega_hRPD_{t-h}  +  \mu_{t}, }\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(RPD_{t}\) is the relative price variance at time \(t\)
\item
  \(|\pi_{t}|\) is the absolute value of aggregate inflation rate at
  time \(t\)
\item
  \(RPD_{t-h}\) are lagged values of RPD to account for its dynamic
  nature, and \(\mu_{t}\) is the error term
\item
  The coefficient \(\beta_1\) captures the effect of inflation on RPD.
  We include three lags of RPD based on Akaike Information Criterion
  (AIC) (and other tests) and to capture potential persistence in RPD.
\item
  For robustness, we repeat this estimation using core inflation
  (excluding food and non-alcoholic beverages, and fuel and electricity)
  instead of headline inflation to account for the potential volatility
  in these components.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Methodology}
\phantomsection\label{methodology-3}
The literature on RPD emphasises that the relationship between inflation
and RPD maybe time-varying due to structural changes in the economy,
monetary policy regimes, and other factors. To capture potential time
variation in the relationship, we also estimate a rolling regression
model with a 24-month and 60-month windows as follows:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-rpd_rolling_reg}{RPD_{t} = \alpha_{t} + \beta_{1,t}|\pi_{t}| + \sum_{h=1}^{3}\omega_{h,t}RPD_{t-h}  +  \mu_{t}, }\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  The coefficients \(\alpha_{t}\), \(\beta_{1,t}\), and \(\omega_{h,t}\)
  vary over time.
\item
  This approach allows us to observe the stability of the coefficients
  over time, which is an indicator of non-linearity in the relationship
  between RPD and inflation.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Methodology}
\phantomsection\label{methodology-4}
Next, we utilise a semi-parametric or partially linear approach to
estimate the optimal level of inflation given the RPD. This approach
allows us to model the relationship between inflation and RPD without
imposing a strict functional form. Following the literature, we first
specify a quadratic (U-shaped) relationship between inflation and RPD as
follows:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-rpd_semi_parametric}{RPD_t = \alpha + \pi_t + \beta_2\pi_t^2 + \sum_{h=1}^{3}\phi_hRPD_{t-h} + \epsilon_t, }\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\beta_2\) captures the curvature of the relationship between
  inflation and RPD
\item
  The optimal inflation rate that minimizes RPD can be derived by taking
  the first derivative of the equation with respect to \(\pi_t\) and
  setting it to zero, yielding:
\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-optimal_inflation}{\pi_t^* = -\dfrac{1}{2\beta_2}.}\end{equation}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Methodology}
\phantomsection\label{methodology-5}
To estimate this optimal inflation we implement a semi-parametric
approach as follows:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-rpd_semi_parametric_f}{RPD_t =  f(\pi_t) + \sum_{h=1}^{3}\phi_hRPD_{t-h} + \epsilon_t, }\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(f(\pi_t)\) is a non-linear smooth differentiable function of
  inflation and the RPD.
\item
  Therefore, the optimal inflation rate can be obtained by finding the
  minimum of the estimated \(f(\pi_t)\) function. \(f(\pi_t)\) is
  estimated in two stages.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Methodology}
\phantomsection\label{methodology-6}
First we estimate \(\lambda_h\) as follows:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-rpd_semi_parametric_stage1}{ RPD_t = \sum_{h=1}^{3}\lambda_h\overline{RPD}_{t-h} + \eta_t, }\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\overline{RPD}_{t-h}\) are the residuals from a non-parametric
  regression of each lag of \(RPD_t\) on \(\pi_t\)
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Methodology}
\phantomsection\label{methodology-7}
In the second stage, we estimate \(f(\pi_t)\) non-parametrically as
follows:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-rpd_semi_parametric_stage2}{\hat{\eta}_t = f(\pi_t) + v_t, }\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(\hat{\eta}_t\) are the residuals from
  Equation~\ref{eq-rpd_semi_parametric_stage1}
\item
  \(f(\pi_t)\) is estimated using a kernel regression method
\item
  The bandwidth (\(h\)) for the kernel estimator is selected using
  cross-validation to minimize the mean squared error.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Methodology}
\phantomsection\label{methodology-8}
\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  As a treatment for extreme values of inflation, we further implement
  the unbounded Gaussian kernel and the outlier-robust Epanechnikov
  kernel.
\item
  In essence, the semi-parametric approach captures the sensitivity of
  RPD to marginal increases in inflation.
\item
  In that case, the optimal inflation rate can be obtained by finding
  the point where the first derivative (\(f'(\pi_t)\)) is equal to zero.
\item
  Therefore, if \(f'(\pi_t) > 0\) (\(f'(\pi_t) < 0\)), then an increase
  in inflation (decrease) leads to an increase (decrease) in the RPD.
  For robustness we estimate the \(g(UIN_t)\) with multiple bandwith
  (\(h\)).
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Methodology}
\phantomsection\label{methodology-9}
To further disentangle the effects of RPD on inflation, we decompose
headline inflation into its expected and unexpected components. As
discussed in the literature section, for example, theory suggests that
inflation uncertainty has a more pronounced effect on RPD due to
information frictions faced by firms when adjusting prices (Lucas 1973;
Hercowitz 1981; Cukierman 1983).

We therefore estimate the following model:

\begin{equation}\phantomsection\label{eq-unexpected}{ RPD_t = \alpha + \beta_0 EIN_t + \beta_1 UIN_t + g(UIN_t) + \sum_{k=1}^{3}\lambda_k RPD_{t-k} + \epsilon_t, }\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
\tightlist
\item
  \(EIN_t\) is the expected inflation rate and \(UIN_t\) is the
  unexpected inflation rate
\item
  \(g(UIN_t)\) is a non-linear function of unexpected inflation
  (\(UIN_t\)).
\item
  We obtain the expected using \textbf{a simple moving average of
  headline inflation, and unexpected inflation as the standard deviation
  of inflation}. The estimation of \(g(UIN_t)\) follows the same
  two-stage semi-parametric approach outlined in
  Equation~\ref{eq-rpd_semi_parametric_stage1} and
  Equation~\ref{eq-rpd_semi_parametric_stage2}. The number of lags is
  the same as in Equation~\ref{eq-rpd_reg}.
\end{itemize}
\end{frame}

\begin{frame}{Methodology}
\phantomsection\label{methodology-10}
Lastly, and some-what novel to the RPD literature, we employ a quantile
on quantile regression approach (Koenker 2017). Quantile on quantile
regressions are robust to outliers and allows us to estimate the regime
aspects of the relationship between RPD and inflation.

For brevity, we do not explain the quantile on quantile estimation
procedure
\end{frame}
